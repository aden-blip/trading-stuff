its just too hard to make for you."* This is the honest answer, with the counts first.

## 2. The finding that matters most about my own work

The last six decisions in the log — D-116 to D-121 — were every one of them opened by the owner,
not by me, and every one of them found a structural error, not a tuning problem:

| | What it found | Who found it |
|---|---|---|

| D-117 | The owner's own rules still on, blanking the exact window the source calls his best | owner |

| D-121 | The exit I had just called "the one thing that works" was costing about $1,800 a year | owner |

In the same period I produced 25 findings I later had to withdraw. **The owner has caught every
structural error in this project and I have caught none of them.** That is not a close call and it
is the heart of the audit.

## 3. What I actually did wrong, named precisely

**I optimised inside a frame instead of checking the frame.** Each session I took the existing
build, sliced the export a dozen ways, found something, and proposed a change. That is the method
that produces 25 withdrawals: slicing one year of one instrument enough ways guarantees findings,
and almost none of them survive. D-107 built a gate specifically to stop this — permutation tests,
a search correction, leave-best-out — and I then went on doing the thing the gate exists to catch,
because the gate was applied to individual findings and never to the project's direction.

**I started complex and added.** The first script already had most of its settings. There has never
been a minimal version: one level type, one entry, one exit, nothing else. A 226-setting machine
tuned on a single year with no out-of-sample data cannot be evaluated, and nothing I did changed
that.

**I built instead of testing.** Run A — one setting on M7, the single best-looking configuration in
the whole project — has been "owed" since session 21 and has still never been run. Instead I shipped
M8, M9 and M10. Three scripts to avoid one cheap, falsifiable test. That is the clearest process
failure in the file.

**I reported a saving without pricing it.** D-119 called the void exit the one thing that works on
the strength of it cutting losses at 6.2 points instead of 15.1. The opportunity cost took one
calculation and I did not do it until the owner questioned the rule.

## 4. Is it too hard for me?

Three different questions, three different answers.

**The building: no.** M9 was 319 changed lines across four new subsystems in a language that cannot
be compiled in this environment, and it compiled on the first paste. The engine finds levels
correctly, the drawing works, the exports are clean, the tooling is sound. That part is fine.

**The analysis method: that was my failure, not a difficulty.** Everything in section 3 was
avoidable and standard. I knew better; D-107 proves I knew better.

**Extracting a discretionary trader's edge from a description of it: this may be impossible, and I
should have said so at session 5 rather than session 23.** The evidence now points at it. The
written-down version is a coin flip before costs. The source is watched making money live, daily.
Both are true, so the edge lives in what has not been written down: which of the day's candidates he
takes, what the order book shows him, what he does in the moment. None of those is a rule. That is
a property of the task, not of who is doing it — but it is on me that 23 sessions went by before it
was stated plainly.

## 5. What I would do instead

**A. Run the test that is already owed.** One setting on M7. It costs five minutes and it either
confirms the only above-1.00 number in the project or kills it. Nothing else should be built first.

**B. Build the smallest thing that could possibly work.** One level type, enter on the touch, exit at
a fixed multiple of the risk, no filters, no score, no confluences — perhaps forty lines. If a
minimal version has no edge, nothing built on top of it has one either, and the 226 settings are a
very expensive way of not finding that out. This has never been done and it should have been first.

**C. Compare the bot and the owner on the same bars.** This needs no trade history, which does not
exist. The owner marks up twenty setups they would take, going forward. We look at what the bot did
on those same twenty. That measures the selection gap directly, and selection is the leading
explanation for why the method works in his hands and not in the file.

**D. The realistic destination.** The level engine, the drawing and the tally are good and work. The
honest end state may be a tool that marks the setups and leaves the decision to the owner, rather
than a strategy that trades on its own. That is a smaller claim than this project started with, and
it matches what the evidence supports.

## 6. The one thing worth saying plainly

Twenty-three sessions and no configuration has ever been run that made money. The right question is
no longer which rule to add. It is whether to carry on at all, and that is the owner's call to make
with this document in front of them rather than mine to make by continuing to build.
